I've been using AI wrong this whole time. For months, I tried getting it to create viral food photos for our burger joint. It kept spitting out fake-looking garbage with weird textures and plastic-looking elements. Then I realised something that changed everything. Instead of asking AI to replace my work, I started using it as my editing coach. I upload my real burger photos to ChatGPT and say: "Help me make this pop." It gives me specific tips: adjust the shadows under the patty, boost the saturation on the lettuce, sharpen the bacon edges. My first result? A Wagyu Bacon Burger that looked like actual food porn instead of AI slop. But here's where it gets interesting. This "AI as coach" philosophy is now how we're starting to operate across everything. We feed our 2850+ Google reviews (4.9 stars) into AI systems that analyses patterns invisible to human eyes. Then it coaches our front-of-house team with surgical precision. Not replacing our people. Making them superhuman at reading customer needs. The insight that changed everything: AI isn't good at creating. It's incredible at teaching. It can't make a perfect burger photo, but it can teach you to see light and composition like a pro photographer. It can't serve customers, but it can turn your team into service ninjas by analysing thousands of data points you'd never notice. We're not just London's highest-rated burger joint anymore. We're staying there because we learned the difference between automation and elevation.

Sakib Ahmed Co-founder of Draper · Own your distribution · 17 Apr 2026 in

The actual work takes 15 minutes but the mental commute between tasks eats the rest of your day. A 15 minute task that felt impossible at 3pm just flows on a quiet Tuesday morning. So we stopped trying to manage it and just stacked everything. six months in and honestly I don't think we could go back.

Sakib Ahmed Co-founder of Draper · Own your distribution · 18 May 2026 in

What happens when you post a real Monet and say it's AI? The coolest art social experiment I've seen in a while. Someone posted a painting online and told people it was AI-generated. Asked them what made it inferior to the original. "Looks like sh*t." "Not as vibrant as Monet's typical palette." "Achieving maybe 20% of the real thing." People went in. It was a real Monet. The actual painting. And honestly the interesting thing here isn't the framing lesson everyone's pulling from this. It's what it tells you about where AI sentiment is right now. People looked at a masterpiece and called it garbage because someone told them a machine made it. They weren't evaluating the painting at all. The word "AI" just did all the work before their eyes even caught up. We run an AI native agency. The founders we work with are producing stuff that's good. Really good. But I know that if you slapped "AI-generated" on some of these posts, the same people who are engaging with them right now would suddenly call it slop. Same post, same words. You just changed what people think wrote it. One of the founders we work with said something about this that stuck with me. If a human writes something mediocre, people think everyone has an off day. If AI writes something mediocre, people think zero effort. That's the Monet experiment in one sentence. I don't think the gap between what AI can actually do and what people think it can do is closing anytime soon.
