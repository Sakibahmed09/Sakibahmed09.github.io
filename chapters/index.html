01 Dieppe

I was born in Dieppe, a small seaside town in northern France with barely a halal takeaway in it. Dad opened the only Indian restaurant in town, and on Saturdays he sold Dragon Ball Z toys off a market stall. I used to ride up front in his van on the supply runs while he worked out what would sell, which was my first lesson in going direct, even if I was six and only there for the toys. The best part of my childhood was the drive to Paris to see my uncle, because Paris meant a grec sandwich. I’ve been chasing that sandwich professionally ever since.

happiness on a plate

We moved to east London when I was eight. Dad and my brother ran an Italian restaurant called Anima D’Italia, Mum spent months perfecting the first halal tiramisu in London for it, and by sixteen I was waiting tables there.

I got 6A* and 5As at GCSE, then BBDD at A-level. Nobody has mentioned the GCSEs since. Goldman Sachs took me anyway.

02 The wrong team five years

Goldman had just launched a technology apprenticeship, first cohort ever, three days at work and two at uni. I was eighteen and meant to go into software engineering. They put me on the voice engineering team instead, where the second youngest person was double my age and nobody had a plan for me.

first cohort, wrong team

So I started turning up to meetings nobody had invited me to. I sat in the corner while Amazon and Cisco pitched Goldman on replacing the phone systems bolted to every trader’s desk, multimillion pound decisions, and I watched pitches fall apart and projects get greenlit and then quietly die six months later. That’s where I worked out the interesting stuff was everything around the engineering. I moved into a proper engineering role eventually and stayed five years.

In my first week I asked where I could pray, and they found a Muslim on the floor to walk me down. He pointed at the prayer room door, said “don’t stay too long in this place,” and left. It took me years to work out he was talking about barakah. I had the job everyone wanted, and somehow I was saving less than my uni friends and had less time than any of them. I never took it as a reason to leave. I took it as a reason to get so good at the job that nobody could question the half hour I took to pray.

03 MiniDeed 2018–2021

the posts from the time

That prayer room is also where I met Mehti. He prayed next to me every day, and one afternoon on the way out I asked how he was doing, just being polite. He heard it as a question about his startup and told me everything. He’d coded it himself and it barely worked. Every like turned into a penny for charity, so anyone could fundraise just by posting, and I couldn’t stop thinking about it. So I asked if I could work on it.

It was two co-founders, one engineer and me. I did users and support, worked out what we should build next, and posted about all of it on Twitter because that felt like the obvious thing to do. We got into Fast Forward, an accelerator in Stratford, and I started skipping uni days to go there instead.

£37 → £2.7k a month

Donations went from £37 a month to £2.7k a month. Then one tweet went viral and Ramadan did the rest, 800,000 taps across twenty-five countries and £26k raised through likes. All of it went to charity and none of it to us, and we never worked out how to make money from it.

What I didn’t see coming was the users guarding the place themselves. Someone would post something within the rules and outside the spirit of it, and other users would step in before we’d even seen it. Nobody asked them to. They cared that much, and that was also our ceiling, because we’d built a room that only fit one kind of person. I watch founders walk into the same trap most weeks now. Donations still come through the app daily, years later.

04 Twenty-five hours 2019

the three parts, as I posted them

In January 2019 I flew to New York with my friend Arefin. I was interrogated and held at JFK for twenty-five hours and then refused entry, with no reason given. I kept it to myself for two years, then wrote it up in three parts.

told two years later

It wasn’t the last time either. In 2025 I spent eight hours in an interrogation room trying to cross into the West Bank. Both are a big part of why I build.

05 The Tiramisu Response Unit 2020

In March 2020 my parents flew to Bangladesh and lockdown was announced while they were in the air. Dad was high risk and stuck there for months, the restaurant had no customers, and the rent didn’t care about pandemics. Me and my brother took it over within the week and started delivering Mum’s tiramisu across London in full trays of 24 slices. I called it the Tiramisu Response Unit, and through the worst months it brought in 40% of our revenue. It’s still the best brand name I’ve come up with, and I’ve had a few since.

still my best brand name

We delivered thousands of pizza slices to hospitals on the side, and it was the busiest time of my life. When we started thinking about selling, a buyer came in, told my dad we were bleeding out, and made an offer so insulting that Dad slammed the table and walked out. We kept the kitchen. A year later Simply Smashed started in the corner of it.

06 140 days 2021

In 2021 I’d just finished five years at Goldman and was leaving for Egypt in a few months to study. So I gave myself 140 days, in public, to launch a food business before I left. The plan was to launch three and see which one lived.

The first was a vegan burger brand. Vegan was everywhere in east London that year and it got real traction, just with a completely different audience to mine. The second was Grec, proper French grec sandwiches, and that was the one I had conviction on. I tested the name with Twitter polls and voice notes and built it in public, and it died on the supply chain. We couldn’t get the meat into the country and it never fed a single person.

never launched

The third was my brother’s idea, and Dad didn’t want to do it. He’d spent decades building a proper Italian restaurant and burgers felt like a step down. He came round when we showed him the numbers. By day 140 it wasn’t close.

07 Simply Smashed 2021–2026

the posts from the time

My brother spent £100 on a grill and started making burgers in the corner of Dad’s restaurant. We pushed the tables away and rented the front out to a flower shop, so people walked through flowers to pick up their burgers. We had no money to do it properly, so we told people what we were building and let them watch us figure it out. My sister-in-law came up with half the recipes we still serve.

Then I went to Egypt to study for nine months while my brother and Dad kept it running. When I came back my brother gave me one rule, which was that my job was to grow it. There were long stretches where I couldn’t tell if any of it was working. One of the front of house guys found out about Goldman mid-shift and just went “you left Goldman Sachs for this?” He wasn’t taking the piss either. He actually felt sorry for me.

By 2025 it was London’s highest rated burger joint on Google, 4.9 stars across 3,000 reviews, and we never spent a pound on paid marketing. Order times went from 50 minutes to 8. The one I still think about is the night I hired a Boris bike to take a missing chicken burger to a customer five minutes away, and he took the bag and shut the door without a word.

4.9 ★, 3,000 reviews

We made a lot of decisions that looked stupid on paper. We closed for the first week of Ramadan, which cost us 30% of the month’s revenue, and built a prayer space where more tables could have gone. We walked away from a site because the landlord wanted us to sell beer. When we got review bombed for supporting Palestine, the community showed up for us.

I left in January 2026. My brother runs it now, and runs it better. In August IFG made a documentary about us that was as much about Dad as the burgers, and it’s the best telling of our story so far.

08 Peruvian Street Kitchen 2025–

the posts from the time

There was a Peruvian place near the office in my Goldman days, with senior bankers in Savile Row suits queuing outside it for chicken. One day I went to see what the fuss was about and the green sauce hit me like a truck. I dropped my Tesco meal deal routine that same day, and five years later I still couldn’t get the taste out of my head.

the sandwich, again

Version one was a disaster, burnt chicken and a perfect sauce. Two months of marinades and custom potato buns later, version sixteen clicked, and the first customer to try it asked how we’d even made it. It went on the Simply Smashed menu as the Peruvian Thigh and sold out in three hours, and then it got its own kitchen in Walthamstow. Simply Smashed meant walking into a market with smashed burgers on every corner and trying to rise above them. With PSK most people had never tried anything like it.

For a while I was running burgers and rice boxes out of one kitchen and taking Draper calls on top, which was absolute chaos. My brother runs this one now too.

09 What the burgers paid for 2022–2024

When I was seventeen I did a summer internship at Jack and Jones in Bangladesh and designed a few t-shirts that actually got printed. Eight years later I went back to make a hoodie with my uncle, a senior graphic designer in the clothing industry, and that became SIMPLYCLO. The Liberation Hoodie sold out in three hours, with all profits going to Gaza, and raised £15k for Palestine. Then I closed it. Bootstrapping clothing meant a production delay at every step, and I’d rather end something cleanly than let it rot.

Simply Foundation is a madrasah and boarding school our family had been quietly running for a couple of years before we said anything about it. Children from underprivileged villages live there while they work towards their hifz, and we cover their tuition, meals and accommodation. In 2024 we made it official and tied it to the shop, so the burgers pay for the school. Four huffadh so far.

10 Thoughtleadr 2024–2025

I was running the highest rated burger joint in London when I took a junior, part-time job at someone else’s agency. Simply Smashed had grown on storytelling and I had no idea why it worked, I was doing it on instinct. Then my brother introduced me to someone doing it for a living, for founders backed by YC and a16z. That was Mustafa. So I asked for a job, part time obviously, because I still had a kitchen to run.

Nobody at the restaurant knew. From a corner of the shop, with noise-cancelling headphones on, I was taking calls with founders who had just raised, then going back out to the pass. What I learned there isn’t on Google and it isn’t in ChatGPT, things like how a founder actually decides what to say, and which stories are ready and which need another month living in someone’s head. Before long they had me training the other writers, which still makes me laugh given how I got in the door. I was Head of Training by the time Mustafa and I left to build our own.

calls from the pass

11 Draper 2025–

the posts from the time

Draper is me, Mustafa and Aaquib, and we run LinkedIn for founders and their teams. I’d been writing the thesis in public for two years before we had a name for it. Content is product and product is content. Builders should be building, and AI should be documenting you cooking.

content is product

The problem underneath it is older than AI. The best thinkers I know don’t post. Their best ideas come out loud, in the ten minutes after a meeting, to one person, and then stay in the room. Every writing tool I’ve used sits at the wrong end of that, an empty box that makes something up from nothing. The one worth building is a witness, something that sits in the room when you say the thing and writes down what you meant, in your voice. I wrote that up properly as The Corridor.

The first year nearly broke me. In October 2025 I fell asleep at the wheel in traffic and drove into the back of the car in front. Nobody was hurt. I was setting up PSK, running the Simply Smashed kitchen and taking Draper calls at six in the morning before service, and I was a bit proud of the hours, which was the tell. So we hired, and stacked every client call into Mondays and Wednesdays so the other three days are for building. Draper doesn’t run on how much sleep I got anymore.

So far that’s 400M+ views for 100+ founders, from YC and a16z-backed companies to VC funds, and north of £20M in pipeline from the content. People called these ideas crazy when I started posting them, and now the industry has names for them. The competition is well funded. One is a founder paying $1m salaries to learn the space, another is a man whose last exit was $780m, and then there’s me and my two bootstrapped boi cofounders.

We’ll win iA.

12 Bilal 2024–

the posts from the time

In October 2024 I posted a question. In Muslim countries you hear the athan from the minaret and you get up and go to the masjid. On your phone it’s one more notification you swipe away. If you heard it out loud at your masjid’s jama’ah time, would it get you through the door? Two weeks later I was testing it on the TV in my front room.

It lived on that TV for the best part of two years while I collected and checked jama’ah times masjid by masjid, and somewhere in there it started getting me to jama’ah I’d have missed, at mosques two minutes away that I never knew were there.

on the App Store 09.10.26

It launched on 11 September 2026, an athan that tells you when to leave for jama’ah at your masjid, not when the prayer starts. Before I’d announced anything it was already going round WhatsApp groups, and 1,400 people were on it in a couple of days. The iPhone app followed on 9 October, alongside the Mac and the TV in your kitchen. It now knows over 24,000 masajid around the world, there’s no sign up, and it’s free and stays free. Android is next, iA.

The logo is a masjid arch, left open for you. Before you knew the way, someone took you to the masjid, and somewhere along the way most of us got lost again. There’s still a place for you in the jama’ah.
