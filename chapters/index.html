01 Dièppe

I was born in Dièppe, northern France. Rural, quiet, and barely a halal takeaway in the town. Dad ran the only Indian restaurant there and it felt like we were the only Asian family for miles. The highlight of my childhood was the drive to Paris to visit my uncle, because Paris meant a grec sandwich from the kebab shops. Happiness on a plate. I have been chasing that sandwich professionally ever since.

this sandwich comes back later

We moved to east London when I was eight, where Dad and my brother ran an Italian restaurant called Anima D’Italia. His signature was the tiramisu and mine was eating it. By sixteen I was waiting tables there.

School gave me my first public humbling. I got 6A* and 5As at GCSE and then BBDD at A-level, and while nobody has mentioned the GCSEs since, I still hear about the A-levels. Goldman Sachs took me anyway.

02 The wrong team five years

They had just launched a technology apprenticeship, the first cohort ever, three days at work and two at uni. I was eighteen and meant to go into a normal software engineering role. Instead I was put on something called the voice engineering team, where the second youngest person was at least double my age and nobody had a plan for me.

first cohort, wrong team

So I started showing up to things. I sat in rooms watching Amazon and Cisco pitch Goldman on replacing the hoots and the turrets, which are the broadcast intercoms and the enormous phone rigs bolted to every trader’s desk. Multimillion pound decisions, and me in the corner acting like I belonged. I watched pitches fall apart and projects get greenlit and then quietly die six months later, and worked out that the interesting part was everything around the engineering. Nobody was checking on me. It would have been the easiest thing in the world to sit at my desk and coast through it. I moved into a proper engineering role eventually and stayed five years.

In my first week I asked where I could pray and they found a Muslim on the floor to walk me down to the prayer room. He pointed at the door, said “don’t stay too long in this place,” and left. It took me years to work out what he meant. That prayer room is where the next chapter starts.

03 MiniDeed 2018–2021

the posts from the time

Mehti prayed next to me every day. One afternoon on the way out I asked how he was doing, and I meant it as a greeting. He heard it as a question about his startup and told me everything. He had coded it himself and it barely worked. Every like turns into a penny for charity, so anyone could fundraise just by posting. I could not stop thinking about it, so I asked if I could work on it.

The company was two co-founders, one engineer and me. I did users and support, worked out what we should build next, and posted about all of it on Twitter because that felt like the obvious thing to do. We got into an accelerator in Stratford and I started skipping the uni days to go there instead.

£37 → £2.7k a month

Donations went from £37 a month to £2.7k a month. Then one tweet went viral and Ramadan did the rest, 800,000 taps across twenty-five countries and £26k raised through likes alone. All of it went to charity and none of it to us, which tells you everything about our business model.

The part I did not see coming was what people did with the place once they were in it. Someone would post something a bit off, within the rules and outside the spirit of the thing, and other users would step in before we had even seen it. They had bought in hard enough to guard it themselves, and that was also the ceiling. We had built a room that only fit one kind of person and everyone else could feel it from the door. We also removed follower counts because they felt toxic, and a lot of people churned because the app stopped giving them the hit they came for. You cannot opt out of the attention economy by pretending it isn’t there. That lesson cost me three years and became the foundation of everything I now do at Draper.

The app still takes donations daily. Sadaqah jariyah beats MRR. He never did tell me how he was doing.

04 Twenty-five hours 2019

the three parts, as I posted them

In January 2019 I flew to New York with my friend Arefin. I was interrogated and held at JFK for twenty-five hours, then refused entry. No reason given. I held the story in for two years, then wrote it up in three parts: the interrogation, the cell, the reflections.

told two years later

That wasn’t the first system I’d been on the wrong end of and it won’t be the last. It’s a big part of why I build. Nobody has to give you permission to start something of your own.

05 The Tiramisu Response Unit 2020

March 2020. My parents got on a flight to Bangladesh and lockdown was announced while they were in the air. Dad was high risk and stuck there for months, the restaurant had no customers, and the rent did not care about pandemics. Me and my brother took it over within the week and launched a tiramisu delivery service, free delivery, the two of us doing everything. I named it the Tiramisu Response Unit. Still the best brand name I have ever come up with and I have had a few since.

still my best brand name

We ran Help Our Heroes alongside it and delivered thousands of pizza slices to hospitals across London, got burgled in the middle of that, and MiniDeed’s Ramadan campaign broke its own records the same month. The busiest stretch of my life. When we started thinking about selling, a buyer told my dad we were bleeding out and made an offer so insulting that Dad slammed the table and walked out, and they smirked and lit cigarettes outside. The kitchen we kept becomes important in chapter seven.

06 140 days 2021

I had just finished at Goldman and was leaving for Egypt in a few months to study. Savings not sustainable, flight booked. So I set a public challenge, 140 days to launch a food concept before I left, and the plan was to launch three of them and find out which one lived.

The first was a vegan burger brand. Vegan was everywhere in east London that year and it genuinely got traction, with a completely different audience to the one I had. The second was Grec, authentic grec sandwiches for east London, the first proper French halal kebab in the UK, and the one I had conviction on. I mapped my unfair advantages, tested the name by Twitter poll and voicenote, and built it in public before that was a phrase. It died on a supply chain. We could not get the meat into the country and it never fed a single person.

never launched

The third was my brother’s idea, and Dad did not want to do it. He had spent decades building a proper Italian restaurant and burgers felt like a step down from that. He came round when we showed him the numbers. By day 140 it was not close. Conviction is a feeling you have about yourself, and the market is never consulted about it.

07 Simply Smashed 2021–2026

the posts from the time

My brother spent £100 on a grill and started making burgers in the corner of Dad’s restaurant, in the kitchen the pandemic left us. Tables pushed away, the front rented out to a flower shop, so people walked through flowers to pick up their burgers. We had no money to do it properly so we told people what we were building and let them watch us figure it out. My sister-in-law wrote half the recipes we still serve.

Then I went to Egypt for nine months and my brother and Dad kept it running. When I came back my brother gave me one rule. Your job is to grow it. There were long stretches where I could not tell whether any of it was working, and I would catch myself wondering if I should have stayed in tech. One of the front of house guys found out about Goldman mid-shift and said “you left Goldman Sachs for this?” He wasn’t taking the piss either. He actually felt sorry for me.

By 2025 it was London’s highest rated burger joint on Google. 4.9 stars across 3,000 reviews, and not a pound of paid marketing behind any of it. My favourite receipts are the small ones. Delivering a missing item myself on a Boris bike in five minutes, and the bloke taking the bag without a word. Asking customers to please keep their socks on, because it had become a communal living room. Order fulfilment going from 50 minutes to 8, which nobody claps for.

4.9 ★, 3,000 reviews

We kept making decisions that looked commercially stupid. We closed for the first week of Ramadan, which is 30% of monthly revenue, and built a prayer space where more covers could have gone. We walked away from a site because the landlord wanted us to sell beer. We got review bombed for Palestine and the community showed up for us. Every one of those paid us back.

I left in January 2026. My brother runs it now, and runs it better. Eight months later IFG made a documentary about it, dug our childhood cassettes out of the attic, and made it about Dad as much as the burgers. A hundred thousand people watched it over a bank holiday weekend, their most watched video in eight years.

08 Peruvian Street Kitchen 2025–

the posts from the time

There was a Peruvian place near the office in my Goldman days with senior bankers queuing outside it in Savile Row suits, for chicken. One day I went to see what the fuss was about and the green sauce hit me like a truck. I threw away my Tesco meal deal routine that day, and five years later I still could not get the taste out of my head. It is the grec sandwich from chapter one again, a plate eaten once and chased for years, except this time I chased it into a business.

the sandwich, again

Version one was a disaster, burnt chicken and a perfect sauce. Two months of marinades and custom potato buns later, version sixteen clicked, and the first customer to try it asked how we had even made it. It went on the Simply Smashed menu as the Peruvian Thigh, then it grew into its own thing. Simply Smashed meant entering a market with a smashed burger place on every corner and trying to be better than all of them. This was the opposite problem. Nobody was asking for it, so the job was to make people want a thing they had never had.

We ran a stealth test before opening properly. Someone spent £100 trying to sabotage it, which I took as a compliment, and a dad tried to buy from us five times in one week and we were shut every time. Running burgers and rice boxes out of one kitchen was chaos. My brother runs this one now too.

09 What the burgers paid for 2022–2024

Around the burgers there was a run of smaller bets. Two are worth telling.

Eight years before SIMPLYCLO I had worked as a graphic designer at a textile factory outside Dhaka, so I went back to the same team to make a hoodie. The Liberation Hoodie, all profits to Gaza, sold out in three hours and raised £15k for Palestine. The best boycott is having somewhere else to go. Then I closed it. Bootstrapping clothing meant a production delay at every step and I would rather end something cleanly than let it rot. Killing a venture well is an underrated skill.

Simply Foundation is a madrasah our family had been quietly running for a couple of years before we said anything about it. We made it official and tied it to the shop, so the burgers pay for the school. Four huffadh so far. The best day of it was two teenage brothers reciting the entire Qur’an from memory in nine hours, finishing two seconds apart.

10 Thoughtleadr 2024–2025

I was running the highest rated burger joint in London when I took a part-time job at someone else’s agency. A junior role. Simply Smashed had grown on storytelling and I had no idea why it worked, I was doing it on instinct, and then my brother introduced me to someone doing the same job for a living, for founders backed by YC and a16z. That was Mustafa. So I asked for a job. Part time, obviously, I still had a kitchen to run.

Nobody at the restaurant knew. I was taking calls with founders who had just raised, from a corner of the shop with noise-cancelling headphones on, then going back out to the pass. The things I learned in that building do not exist on Google and they are not in ChatGPT. How a founder actually decides what to say, and which stories are ready and which need another month living in someone’s head. Before long they had me training the other writers, which still makes me laugh given how I got in the door, and I left as Head of Training.

calls from the pass

The part nobody can hand off is getting the story out of someone’s head, and that turned out to be the job. Mustafa and I left because AI was about to take the floor out of the model. Once a language model can produce the deliverable, an agency that sells deliverables has a problem, and the value moves to whoever owns the method.

11 Draper 2025–

the posts from the time

In late 2025 I came back to tech. Draper is me, Mustafa and Aaquib turning founders into industry authorities, with content treated as what it actually is now, part of the product.

I’d been writing the thesis in public for two years without naming the company. Building in public is part of the product, and storytelling is the most valuable skill of the AI era. Content compounds alongside the product or the product stays unknown. Now we build the machine that does it properly.

content is product

The problem underneath all of it is older than AI. The best thinkers I know do not post. Their best ideas come out loud, in the ten minutes after a meeting, to one person, and then stay in the room. Every writing tool I have used sits at the wrong end of that, an empty box that invents something out of nothing. The one worth building is a witness, something that sits in the room when you say the thing and writes down what you meant, in your voice. I wrote that up properly as The Corridor.

Nine months in, the industry has found names for what we were doing, forward deployed content engineers and AI native services firms, and last winter people told me these ideas were mad. A hundred-odd founders and north of £20M in pipeline for them so far. The competition is well funded. One is a founder paying $1m salaries to learn the space, another is a man whose last exit was $780m. Then there is me and my two bootstrapped boi cofounders.

We’ll win iA.

12 Bilal 2024–

the posts from the time

In 2024 I posted a question. In Muslim countries the athan comes off the minaret and you get up and go. On your phone it is one more notification you swipe away. Would hearing it out loud, at your masjid’s jama’ah time, actually get people through the door? A fortnight later I was testing it on the TV in my front room.

It stayed there for the best part of two years while I collected and checked the jama’ah times of over 2,500 masajid, and somewhere in that it started getting me to prayers I would have missed, at mosques two minutes away that I never knew were there.

launched 11.09.26

It launched properly on 11 September 2026. An athan that tells you when to leave for jama’ah at your masjid, not when the prayer starts, on your phone, your Mac and the TV in your kitchen. No sign up, free, and it stays free. Before I had announced anything it was already going round WhatsApp groups and had 1,400 people on it in a couple of days.

The logo is a masjid arch, left open for you. The feeling I want it to give you is your dad holding your hand and walking you to the masjid when you were eight. Somewhere along the way most of us lost that. The guiding hand is still there and waiting for you.
